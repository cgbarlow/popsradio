# Other NZ B2B, community and government segments for "Grandad's radio" (one-button Wi-Fi push-to-talk)

Scope: NZ organisational buyers other than rest homes, retirement villages, hospitals, hospices, and disability and home support, which other researchers cover. Research date: 15 November 2026. Method note: WebFetch was blocked for www.tpk.govt.nz, www.educationcounts.govt.nz and figure.nz (DNS not allowed in this session), so figures from those hosts come from search-engine extracts and are marked [verify]. Stats NZ / Figure.NZ business counts are at February 2025 (released 30 Oct 2025). School counts are at 1 July 2025.

## 1. Seniors' social and community housing (Kāinga Ora, council pensioner housing, community housing providers)

### Takeaway
Seniors' social housing is the best-fitting B2B segment found. It is a few dozen landlords with concentrated stock, roughly 1,000–2,500 units each in the big metros, plus many small council portfolios of 30–200 units. Tenants live alone in clustered one-bedroom units, and most have Wi-Fi or could get it. The main gap is that there is no national count of "older persons' units". A bottom-up estimate puts dedicated council and CHP seniors' stock at roughly 8,000–12,000 units nationally [estimate], plus thousands of Kāinga Ora homes designated for older tenants.

- Councils typically offer small one-bedroom units or bedsits at below-market rents to people aged 60–65+ on low incomes, called "housing for older people" or "pensioner housing" — [Senior Guide](https://www.seniorguide.co.nz/articles/renting-in-later-life).

### Inferences
- **Fit: strong.** Clustered single-occupant older tenants suit a "neighbour / warden / family" channel. A complex of 10–40 units maps naturally onto the 10-radio channel limit, for example one channel per block plus a tenancy-manager radio. The device is not an alarm, so pitch it as social connection and tenant engagement, not safety monitoring, and expect landlords to ask exactly that question.
- **How they buy:** Large providers (Haumaru, OCHT, Te Toi Mahana) run tenancy-sustainment and wellbeing programmes and would buy through a pilot, then a procurement or board decision, often co-funded by a grant (Lottery, Office for Seniors, council community grants). Small council portfolios (under 200 units) buy through council community or positive-ageing budgets and are slow, annual-plan driven.
- **Wi-Fi is the blocker.** Many low-income older tenants have no home broadband. A landlord-provided communal or per-unit Wi-Fi (some CHPs already do this) or a bundled hotspot would be needed. This is a deployment cost the provider would have to carry [inference].
- Rough sizing [estimate]: the dedicated older-person council and CHP units in the table total about 4,600. Adding the many unlisted district council portfolios (most of NZ's 67 territorial authorities historically held pensioner housing, though many have divested) gives perhaps 8,000–12,000 dedicated units nationally. If 5–10% adopted, that is about 400–1,200 radios. The segment is modest in volume but valuable as a reference customer.

### Gaps
- No authoritative national count of council-owned older persons' units by region. The NZCCSS or Local Government NZ may hold one. Wellington City Council's older-person share within the Te Toi Mahana portfolio was not found.
- Kāinga Ora tenant age breakdown and current count of older-person-designated homes: not found. It would need an OIA or annual-report appendix.
- OCHT older-tenant share and Dunedin's current 2025–26 unit count were not verified from primary sources.

## 2. Māori and Pacific providers (kaumātua housing, iwi health and social services, Whānau Ora, Pacific)

### Takeaway
Whānau Ora was restructured from 1 July 2025 into four commissioning agencies that choose their own providers. They plus several hundred kaupapa Māori and Pacific health and social providers are a credible channel, especially for papakāinga or kaumātua housing and whānau channels. Cultural fit features (te reo Māori, and Pacific-language voice prompts and labels) would be a differentiator. Dollar sizing was not obtainable because the TPK site was blocked.

### Cited Findings
- The original Whānau Ora Commissioning Agencies (Te Pou Matakana, Te Pūtahitanga o Te Waipounamu, Pasifika Futures) ran from 2014 to 30 June 2025. Four new agencies started on 1 July 2025 across four regions. TPK says overall funding was not reduced and is allocated to regions of greatest need using the Census 2023 Deprivation Index — [TPK commissioning agencies (search extract) [verify]](https://www.tpk.govt.nz/en/nga-putea-me-nga-ratonga/whanau-ora/commissioning-agencies); [RNZ 1 May 2025](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/political/559554/te-puni-kokiri-reveals-new-whanau-ora-commissioning-agencies).
- The four agencies are Rangitāmiro (North Island north of and including Taupō), Te Rūnanga o Toa Rangatira / Māhutonga (lower North Island), Te Tauraki (Ngāi Tahu subsidiary, South Island) and The Cause Collective / Tātou Collective (Pacific, national) — [RNZ 1 May 2025](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/political/559554/te-puni-kokiri-reveals-new-whanau-ora-commissioning-agencies); [RNZ Ngāti Toa agency](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/national/565557/ngati-toa-launch-new-agency-to-distribute-whanau-ora-funds); [RNZ Cause Collective, 12 Mar 2025](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/pacific/544534/new-era-for-whanau-ora-cause-collective-takes-the-helm-in-supporting-pasifika).
- Commissioning agencies decide which service providers they engage. Some former providers, for example Te Kōhao Health, faced closure when their funding ended — [TPK (search extract) [verify]](https://www.tpk.govt.nz/en/a-matou-whakaarotau/whanau-ora/commissioning/commissioning-agencies); [RNZ 10 Mar 2025](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/544361/a-destruction-of-whanau-ora-by-stealth-providers).
- In late June 2025 TPK began an independent review into allegations that public money was misused by former agencies — [RNZ 27 Jun 2025](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/565354/allegations-public-money-used-by-whanau-ora-for-electioneering-to-be-investigated).
- The Healthpoint directory lists 368 kaupapa Māori and 86 Pacific community health and social service entries. These are listings, not a verified provider count — [Healthpoint kaupapa Māori](https://healthpoint.co.nz/community-health-and-social-services/kaupapa-maori-community-health-social/?services=200); [Healthpoint Pasifika](https://healthpoint.co.nz/community-health-and-social-services/pasifika-community-health-social/?services=40).
- Kaumātua housing examples: Ngāti Whakaue's Manawa Gardens (Rotorua), 240 affordable rentals over three stages including purpose-built kaumātua units — [Te Rau Ora](https://terauora.com/poipoia-kaumatua/); a Māori-led health and housing precinct with kaumātua housing beside Middlemore — [Waatea News](https://waateanews.com/?p=166893); Te Rūnaka o Awarua kaumātua housing (Bluff), studied in BMC Public Health, which notes many iwi and Māori providers are developing kaumātua and intergenerational papakāinga — [BMC Public Health 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s12889-024-18771-9).
- The Selwyn Foundation has partnered with iwi, including a kawenata with Ngāti Whātua Ōrākei, on kaumātua wellbeing — [Selwyn Foundation](https://selwynfoundation.org.nz/careers/about-us/news/the-selwyn-foundation-partners-with-iwi-to-elevate-kaumātua-wellbeing).
- The Ministry for Pacific Peoples' Pacific Healthy Homes programme targets older Pacific people with chronic conditions through Pacific providers — [MPP](https://mpia.govt.nz/programmes/pacific-healthy-homes).
- There were 412 kōhanga reo in 2025 (down 2) — [Education Counts (search extract) [verify]](https://www.educationcounts.govt.nz/statistics/services).

### Inferences
- **Fit: medium to strong, for social connection.** Whānau channels (kaumātua, mokopuna, whānau overseas over Wi-Fi) fit Whānau Ora's whānau-centred model. Papakāinga clusters behave like the seniors' housing segment above. Te reo Māori prompts (for example "Kōrero", "Kua tukuna") and kaumātua co-design would matter more than price.
- **How they buy:** through commissioning agencies' discretionary or innovation funding, provider contracts, iwi social and health arms (often with their own trust income), and Te Puni Kōkiri / HUD papakāinga programmes. Relationship-led and slow; expect a requirement for Māori data-sovereignty assurances. E2E encryption and NZ or Australian data hosting would help [inference].
- The 2025 restructure and the misuse review create near-term budget uncertainty. Approach the new agencies as programme-innovation partners, not procurement targets.

### Gaps
- Whānau Ora annual appropriation (historically about $150m+/yr, unverified here) and per-agency allocations: TPK site blocked [verify].
- No national count of kaumātua housing units or iwi housing providers was found. HUD's Māori housing (Te Kahui / MAIHI) data might have one.
- Number of Pacific providers contracted by the Tātou Collective: not found.

## 3. NGOs serving older people (Age Concern, St John Caring Caller, Red Cross, Neighbourhood Support, church social services, dementia organisations)

### Takeaway
The befriending and visiting NGOs reach low thousands of isolated older people each: Age Concern's AVS historically had about 2,300 volunteer visitors, and St John Caring Caller has about 800 volunteers and 1,250+ clients. These programmes are structurally the closest fit, being check-in and loneliness work. They are a better channel or partner than a large direct buyer, and would need grant money to put devices in clients' homes.

### Cited Findings
- Age Concern NZ has 34 member councils (own site, undated). The Charities Register and earlier sources say 40 locations, members, branches and associates. Local Age Concerns are independent incorporated societies — [Age Concern NZ](https://ageconcern.org.nz/?p=1270); [Charities Register CC10939](https://register.charities.govt.nz/Charity/CC10939); [Scoop 2020](https://m.scoop.co.nz/stories/CU2007/S00133/age-concern-new-zealand-welcomes-new-look-board-via-zoom.htm).
- Age Concern Accredited Visiting Service (AVS), historical national figure (about 2013): 2,300 volunteer visitors made 75,000+ visits and 15,000 supportive phone calls a year — [Scoop c.2013](https://scoop.co.nz/stories/GE1306/S00068/lonely-older-people-gain-company-from-volunteer-visitors.htm).
- Age Concern Canterbury's AVS (Canterbury, West Coast, Timaru): 206 registered volunteers and 5,223 visits in 2022 — [Keeping On, May 2023](https://issuu.com/ageconcerncanterbury/docs/keeping_on_may_2023/s/25299187).
- St John Caring Caller: free phone befriending (0800 4 CALLER); "more than 800 volunteers… regularly connecting with over 1,250 clients" (undated release announcing a Hindi-language service). Another St John article muddles the 800 figure as clients — [St John Hindi Caring Caller](https://www.stjohn.org.nz/news--info/news--articles/st-john-launches-hindi-caring-caller-service/); [St John holiday article](https://www.stjohn.org.nz/news--info/news--articles/st-johns-caring-service-a-phone-call-away-this-holiday-season). ASB staff are a corporate volunteer team for Caring Caller — [ASB 2018](https://asb.co.nz/documents/media-centre/media-releases/asb-celebrates-its-dedicated-team-of-caring-callers.html).

### Inferences
- **Fit: strong conceptually, weak commercially.** Caring Caller and AVS rely on volunteers' own phones. A radio would let a client "press to say hello" to a volunteer or coordinator, but it changes the service model and raises safeguarding and boundary questions for volunteers (always-on contact). A pilot partnership, for example 20–50 clients per region funded by a grant, is more realistic than a sale.
- **Channel value:** about 34–40 local Age Concerns plus St John's national programme give referral and distribution reach to isolated seniors, including family buyers. A co-branded "family channel" referral could matter more than direct NGO purchase.
- The St John Hindi service shows demand for language-specific connection. Multilingual voice prompts (Hindi, Samoan, Tongan, Mandarin, te reo) could matter here.

### Gaps
- No current (2024–26) national AVS client or volunteer numbers. The Age Concern NZ annual report was not retrieved.
- No figures found for Red Cross (community programmes), Neighbourhood Support (groups and members), Meals on Wheels, church social services (NZCCSS members), or Alzheimers NZ / Dementia NZ reach. These remain gaps.

## 4. Funders (Lottery Grants Board, Office for Seniors, councils, philanthropic trusts, corporates)

### Takeaway
The realistic funders for device pilots are the Lottery Grants Board's Lottery Community committees (about $398m distributed in 2024/25; about 1,250 grants in a single 2025/26 round) and the Office for Seniors Age-friendly Fund ($5k–$20k grants). These suit pilot-scale purchases of roughly 40–400 radios, not national roll-outs.

### Cited Findings
- The Lottery Grants Board allocated $398,029,957 of lottery profits in 2024/25 — [LGB Annual Report 2024/25](https://www.communitymatters.govt.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/103852/Lottery-Grants-Board-Annual-Report-2024-25.pdf). For 2026/27 it allocated $311,030,630 to distribution committees — [DIA Community Matters, committee allocations](https://www.communitymatters.govt.nz/lottery-grants-board-committee-allocations).
- A secondary source reports $235.9m to committees for 2025/26, and 1,253 grants worth $53.3m across 12 Lottery Community committees in 2025/26 round one — [Grantsights (secondary) [verify]](https://grantsights.com/blog/lottery-grants-board-nz); official per-round records are at [Community Matters round one](https://www.communitymatters.govt.nz/lottery-community/202526-round-one-lottery-national-committee-record-of-grants).
- The Office for Seniors Age-friendly Fund (formerly Community Connects) offers $5,000–$20,000 grants to councils, community organisations and non-profits for age-friendly projects. Applications for the current round close 30 September 2026. A July 2025 independent evaluation found real community benefits. Office for Seniors also administers two new Selwyn Foundation Ageing Well grants — [Office for Seniors](https://www.osc.govt.nz/latest-news/grants-now-available-for-community-projects-that-support-older-people); [Selwyn Foundation](https://selwynfoundation.org.nz/careers/about-us/news/new-grants-to-support-research-and-community-innovation-for-ageing-well-launched-by-the-selwyn-foundation).

### Inferences
- **How to use them:** a provider (an Age Concern, a CHP or a marae) applies, and the grant buys radios plus a year of channels. At NZ$50–125 per radio plus about NZ$35–99 per channel, a $20k Age-friendly grant funds roughly 120–300 radios. A typical Lottery Community grant could do the same or more [inference].
- Regional philanthropic trusts (Rātā Foundation in Canterbury, Nelson and Marlborough; Foundation North in Auckland and Northland; Trust Waikato) and council positive-ageing strategies are plausible co-funders, but no figures were retrieved in this pass.

### Gaps
- No 2025/26 grant totals were found for Rātā Foundation, Foundation North or Trust Waikato, or for council positive-ageing budgets.
- No corporate sponsorship programmes for seniors' tech were identified, apart from ASB's volunteering for Caring Caller.

## 5. Workplace push-to-talk over Wi-Fi (hospitality, retail, hotels, ECE and school staff, farms, marinas, events, churches, warehouses, clinics)

### Takeaway
On raw numbers the workplace market is the largest: 617,334 enterprises (Feb 2025), including 26,070 in accommodation and food services. Fit for this product is weak to moderate. The incumbents are cheap licence-free UHF CB radios (about NZ$35–190 each, no Wi-Fi or subscription needed, range independent of the network) and free or cheap smartphone PTT (Microsoft Teams Walkie Talkie, Zello). A consumer-style one-button Wi-Fi device wins only in niches: indoor sites with good Wi-Fi where UHF is awkward, multi-site linking over the internet, or very simple use by non-technical or older volunteers (churches, op-shops, community halls, small motels).

### Cited Findings
- Total NZ enterprises at Feb 2025 were 617,334 (2024: 614,202; 2023: 607,191) — [Figure.NZ / Stats NZ [verify]](https://figure.nz/chart/IW0xisEu03j4YW09-kvSu2UMTHjeSwClL).

### Inferences
- **Honest fit assessment by sub-segment** [inference]:

| Sub-segment | Fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Cafés and restaurants (front to kitchen) | Weak–moderate | Short range, so cheap UHF CB or a bell works. A 10-device cap is fine, but there is no earpiece or hands-free mode, which staff need. |
| Hotels / motels (about 3,000–4,000 accommodation businesses [estimate]) | Moderate for small motels | Wi-Fi is already everywhere on site. Owner-to-housekeeping or owner-to-elderly-parent use. Larger hotels use Motorola or Hytera DMR with earpieces. |
| Retail stores | Weak | Need discreet earpieces; UHF or Teams covers it. |
| Schools / ECE staff | Moderate | Good Wi-Fi across campus; useful for duty teachers or office to classroom. But a 10-radio cap, no lockdown or alarm role, and staff already use phones and UHF. |
| Farms / orchards | Weak | Paddocks lack Wi-Fi; UHF CB is entrenched and works off-grid. Possible exception: house-to-shed or house-to-elderly-parent if Wi-Fi reaches. |
| Marinas, events | Weak | Outdoor coverage; UHF dominates. |
| Churches, op-shops, community halls, marae | Moderate–strong | Non-technical, often older volunteers; one button suits them; low volume per site. |
| Warehouses | Weak | Rugged devices, headsets and scanners with PTT are already used. |
| Small clinics | Weak–moderate | Indoor Wi-Fi suits it, but practice-management messaging and intercoms exist. |

- The product's real differentiators in B2B are internet-range (multi-site) PTT with no phone and no app, and E2E encryption. Its weaknesses are the 10-per-channel cap, no hands-free or earpiece mode, Wi-Fi dependence, and no ruggedisation. It should not be positioned against UHF CB on price or range.

## 6. Schools and ECE (counts by region; 2024 phone ban; special schools)

### Takeaway
There are 2,536 schools (July 2025, including 36 specialist schools) and about 4,400 licensed ECE services (June 2024, including 412 kōhanga reo in 2025). The 2024 "Phones Away for the Day" rule covers students, not staff, so it does not directly create demand. A screen-free walkie for younger children (school to home or class to class) is a possible but contentious angle. Specialist schools are the best education fit.

### Inferences
- **Schools as staff-comms buyers: moderate fit.** A typical school might use 5–10 radios (office, principal, duty teachers, caretaker), which fits one channel. Purchase is a principal or business-manager decision from the operations grant, low-ticket (under $1,500), so it can be sold direct or through education resellers. Competition is UHF CB and staff phones.
- **Specialist schools (36) and satellite classes:** stronger fit. There are students with communication or mobility needs, and staff spread across satellite units hosted in mainstream schools that share Wi-Fi.
- **Phone ban angle:** the ban targets student phones; a radio a student carries could breach school rules unless exempted. Do not lead with "phone-ban workaround". At most, position it as a home-use, screen-free family device for children [inference].
- **ECE:** small sites with indoor and outdoor areas. Staff currently shout, or use UHF or phones. Weak to moderate fit; there are many sites (about 4,400) but tiny budgets.

## 7. Other credible fits (refuges, mental-health community residences)

### Takeaway
Women's refuges (41 affiliated; about 55 MSD-funded in total) and supported-living residences are small, plausible niches for in-house staff or resident channels. The safety context is a caution: this is not an alarm, and E2E encryption is a plus but a misused device is a risk. Fit is weak to moderate, and these are low priority.

### Cited Findings
- The National Collective of Independent Women's Refuges is the umbrella body for 41 affiliated refuges (2023/24 annual report; repeated in 2025 research) — [Women's Refuge AR 2024](https://womensrefuge.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Annual-Report-2024-ONLINE.pdf); [Women's Refuge 2025 research](https://womensrefuge.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/SWSH-reframing-risk-and-safety-research-WEB.pdf).
- MSD funds and partners with 55 women's refuges, including 14 non-affiliated as at May 2025 — [MSD Women's Refuge](https://msd.govt.nz/about-msd-and-our-work/work-programmes/family-and-sexual-violence/family-violence-services/womens-refuge.html); [MSD OIA July 2025](https://msd.govt.nz/documents/about-msd-and-our-work/publications-resources/official-information-responses/2025/july/10072025-fte-funding-provided-to-affiliated-and-non-affiliated-women-s-and-men-s-refuges.pdf).
- Women's Refuge reported rising demand in November 2025 — [RNZ Checkpoint 14 Nov 2025](https://www.rnz.co.nz/national/programmes/checkpoint/audio/2019012741/increase-in-need-to-escape-abusive-homes-shocks-women-s-refuge).

### Inferences
- Refuges: a possible use is staff to safe-house comms, or a "call the advocate" button for residents. But devices must not be traceable or misusable by perpetrators, and a not-an-alarm product could be misunderstood in a crisis. Weak fit unless co-designed.
- Mental-health and addiction community residences (for example Emerge Aotearoa, Pathways, Kāhui Tū Kaha) could use it for resident to night-staff contact. These are mostly under the disability and supported-living umbrella that other researchers cover, so they are not sized here.

### Gaps
- No count of mental-health community residential beds or houses was retrieved.
- No count of refuge safe houses (as opposed to refuge organisations) was found.
